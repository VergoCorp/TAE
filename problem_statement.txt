Problem Statement for Thermoacoustic Engine (TAE) Simulation Project

Goal:
The goal of this project is to develop a software simulation for a standing-wave thermoacoustic engine (TAE), focusing on the cooling performance of the cold heat exchanger (CHX). The simulation must accurately model the TAE's behavior, aligning with experimental data from Mahlalela et al. (2024) (onset time: 136 s, volumetric velocity: 5.92 m/s, resonance frequency: ~114 Hz) and theoretical principles from Geng Chen et al. (2025). The software should integrate with a user interface (UI) in Windsurf IDE, allowing dynamic input of materials, working fluids, coolants, and thicknesses, and produce performance metrics such as onset time, volumetric velocity, resonance frequency, and temperature difference.

Instructions for What We Are Trying to Build:
1. Model a standing-wave TAE with the following components: left resonator tube, hot heat exchanger (HHX), stack (honeycomb ceramic), cold heat exchanger (CHX), and right resonator tube with an outlet.
2. Implement Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) to capture non-linear behavior:
   - Heat equation for the stack: ρ c_p ∂T/∂t = k ∂²T/∂x²
   - Wave equation for the resonator: ∂²p/∂t² = c² ∂²p/∂x²
3. Use finite difference methods in Python to solve the PDEs, ensuring numerical stability (e.g., satisfying the CFL condition).
4. Integrate UI inputs to dynamically adjust material properties (e.g., thermal conductivity, density), working fluids (e.g., speed of sound), coolants (e.g., convective heat transfer coefficient), and thicknesses (e.g., affecting thermal resistance and effective length).
5. Visualize the TAE design using PyVista via an MCP server in Windsurf IDE, rendering components like the stack and resonator as 3D plots (e.g., temperature or pressure fields).
6. Ensure the simulation aligns with experimental data: onset time (136 s), volumetric velocity (5.92 m/s), frequency (~114 Hz), and trends from Chen et al. (e.g., water cooling improves performance).

Appendix: Scope and Development of a Thermoacoustic Engine Simulation in Windsurf IDE
Introduction

Thermoacoustic engines (TAEs) represent a promising technology for sustainable energy conversion, leveraging the thermoacoustic effect to transform heat into acoustic energy with minimal moving parts. This appendix outlines the scope and development process of a software simulation for a standing-wave TAE, focusing on the cooling performance of the cold heat exchanger (CHX). The project integrates experimental data from Mahlalela et al. (2024), theoretical insights from Geng Chen et al. (2025), and modern computational tools within the Windsurf IDE environment. Our objective is to create a robust simulation that accurately models the TAE’s behavior, supports dynamic user inputs, and facilitates visualization, ultimately contributing to the optimization of TAE designs for enhanced efficiency and performance.

Project Goal

The primary goal is to develop a software simulation that models the dynamics of a standing-wave TAE, with a specific emphasis on the CHX’s cooling performance. The simulation must align with experimental results from Mahlalela et al. (2024), which report an onset time of 136 seconds, a volumetric velocity of 5.92 m/s, and a resonance frequency of approximately 114 Hz. Additionally, it should reflect theoretical trends from Geng Chen et al. (2025), who demonstrated that water cooling enhances TAE efficiency (2.31%) and pressure amplitude (45 Pa) compared to alternatives like heat pipes or no cooling. The simulation is designed to integrate with a user interface (UI) in Windsurf IDE, allowing users to input materials, working fluids, coolants, and thicknesses, and output key performance metrics such as onset time, volumetric velocity, resonance frequency, and temperature difference across the stack.

UI Integration in Windsurf IDE: The simulation integrates with a UI in Windsurf IDE, allowing users to specify:
Materials (e.g., copper, steel, aluminum, plastic) and their properties (thermal conductivity, density, specific heat).

Visualization with PyVista: To enhance analysis, the project leverages PyVista for 3D visualization, integrated via an MCP server in Windsurf IDE. PyVista renders components like the stack and resonator, visualizing temperature or pressure fields as 3D plots. Technical Sketch Refinement: A technical sketch of the TAE is iteratively refined using DeepSeek in Windsurf IDE. The sketch depicts the TAE’s cross-section, with components colored (red for HHX/CHX, blue for stack, gray for tubes), dimension labels (20 mm, 85 mm, 50 mm), and details like the stack’s honeycomb pattern and purple sensor channels. The process involves correcting DeepSeek’s renderings to match the original design, focusing on proportions, colors, and annotations.
Challenges and Solutions

Several challenges emerged during development:

Non-Linear Behavior: Initially, algebraic formulas were considered, but the TAE’s non-linear dynamics necessitated PDEs, which were implemented to capture spatial and temporal variations accurately.
Numerical Stability: A runtime error (“Time step too large, violates CFL condition”) was resolved by reducing the time step to 0.00001 s, ensuring stability in the finite difference method.
Visualization Integration: Setting up PyVista in Windsurf IDE required configuring an MCP server, addressing dependencies (e.g., VTK, FastAPI), and ensuring headless rendering compatibility.
Sketch Accuracy: DeepSeek’s renderings of the TAE sketch required multiple iterations to correct proportions, colors, and details, such as the stack’s honeycomb pattern and dimension labels.
Expected Outcomes

The simulation aims to:

Replicate experimental results: onset time (136 s), volumetric velocity (5.92 m/s), and frequency (~114 Hz).
Reflect theoretical trends: water cooling’s superior performance, as per Chen et al.
Provide a flexible tool for researchers to explore TAE designs by varying materials, fluids, and configurations via the UI.
Enable 3D visualization of TAE dynamics, enhancing understanding of temperature and pressure distributions.
Conclusion

This project bridges experimental and theoretical research on TAEs, delivering a comprehensive simulation tool within Windsurf IDE. By modeling non-linear dynamics, integrating user inputs, and visualizing results, it supports the optimization of TAE designs, contributing to advancements in sustainable energy technologies. The iterative refinement of the technical sketch ensures accurate documentation, while the use of modern tools like PyVista and DeepSeek enhances the project’s analytical and visual capabilities.
